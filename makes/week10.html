Research Notes

In doing my research on content moderation, I found that the most helpful sources were from The Verge, Time, Wired, and The Bureau of Investigative Journalism. These websites gave good overviews of the content moderation job and the emotional toll it takes on employees. They were all very thorough in their research: The Verge studied a Phoenix content moderation site working with Cognizant and Facebook, the Bureau found statistics on different tech companies and stories of companies around the world, Wired focused on a few specific people and their stories (which included a tech CEO, offering a new perspective), and Time uses qualitative data and interviews to do a bit of all three. These articles helped me get a better understanding of what content moderation is, and the emotional toll it takes on workers.

The hardest information to find was specific statistics to list on my infographic. I thought numbers would be interesting and eye-catching for the reader, but I found that statistics that were agreed upon across the sites were very uncommon. This showed me that content moderation as a job has a much larger scope than I had previously understood. With so many different locations and people across countries, it becomes difficult to tell one story of the content moderator experience. In addition, content moderation is a side of AI that tech companies do not like to show, as that would cause them to openly take responsibility for it. This inhibits journalists from gathering quantitative data about the process, thus limiting the available statistics.

On the other hand, I was surprised by the amount of information that I could find. While the data was mostly qualitative, it was also specific, emotional, and honest. Most interviewees were kept anonymous for their own safety, but the fact that there was so much known about the horrors of content moderation already surprised me. I wondered why this isn't even more well known, and how tech companies can hide it so well.

Design Statement

I chose an infographic because it is a simple and clear way to get information across quickly. I tried to do this in a very emotional and visual narrative, telling the story and arguing my perspective through both the text and the images. The text draws the reader's attention to the divide between content moderators and the tech companies that employ them, and emphasizes the danger of their labor and of fighting back. The images also support this narrative; there is a trail of blood drops leading the reader from the problem through the whole story, and stopping at the point where the reader takes action. This is meant to represent the trauma that content moderators go through, and inspire the reader to speak up. In addition, I felt it was important that the content moderators were not represented as a graphic like the rest of the visuals. Because my audience is readers who are unfamiliar with the idea of content moderation and its downsides, I wanted to avoid dehumanizing the moderators with a graphic. I used a real image of contractors in the Philippines so that the readers were drawn to that part of the infographic, and would feel both uncomfortable and emphatic about what they were learning. I hoped this would help the narrative stay with them, and encourage them to speak out, now that they see real people affected by these stories. I also wanted them to feel uncomfortable in order to help them critically consider the choices they make when using AI. Similar to Hicks' key concepts, my infographic encourages the reader to question what goes into AI, and who has the most power to shape the narrative of technology.
