Geography of AI Power

A Google My Maps project tracing data centers, headquarters, resources, and AI supply chains.

The Map

A visualization of how resources are collected for data centers.

Open Google My Maps

Visual Design

  • Platform: Google My Maps
  • Black: headquarters
  • Yellow: current Google data centers
  • Dark yellow: in development Google data centers
  • Dark blue: current Meta data centers
  • Purple: in development Meta data centers
  • Orange: Meta's sand sources
  • Blue: global sources of major AI center resources

Research Documentation

For Google and Meta's official locations, such as their headquarters and data centers, I referred to their company websites. I was a bit surprised to see that these websites openly shared information about current and future data centers. I have always been exposed to data centers in the context of the damage that they do to the environment and the people who live nearby, such as concerns surrounding the use of drinking water. However, Google and Meta were open about their locations, and offered a lot of information about the positive impacts (offering jobs, expanding technology, environmentally conscious choices) of their centers. While surprising, this makes sense; the companies want to market their presence as a good thing, and they're writing the narrative so that readers only see the benefits of their presence.

The hardest thing to find was the mining sites that the companies source their raw materials from, especially for Meta. Because the materials come from third-party companies and the global supply chain rather than mines owned by Google or Meta, there were no easy sources to turn to. This is especially complicated by the fact that the resources are often sourced unethically, considering the dangerous conditions in globally important mines like the ones in the Democratic Republic of Congo. This means that companies likely avoid saying who their suppliers are, because the topic is harder to portray positively than data centers are. Instead, they emphasize the global supply chain that is used. Meta made the research especially difficult, because many of the sources I could find were emphasizing how Meta recycles material and aims to be as sustainable as possible. While I believe this is true and respect Meta's efforts, I also know that the materials have to be somewhat connected to the global markets, and this made my research more difficult. Most of the sources I used were short news articles about deals or collaborations with mining companies for both Google and Meta, as well as research about the world's biggest mines for the materials data centers need.

Artist Statement

My map demonstrates the global distribution of AI power. Many data centers are located in the US and in Europe, and more are being developed, but the mining is outsourced to the global south. Those who live near data centers and headquarters in the global north are offered job opportunities and AI access by the tech companies controlling the labor distribution. On the other hand, the global south bears the costs of exploitation for resources and environmental impacts of mining.

The division of layers-Google, Meta, and then mining sites-highlight how many of these mines are not connected to one company, but instead impact a global supply chain that multiple tech companies profit from. This is also reflected in the data and sources used, which show limited information on exact locations of mines or resource providers, and only statistical highlights or general references to important locations. This means that the clear links between the companies and the mines are missing, likely because that would place more direct blame on the companies for the inequalities and exploitation fostered by mineral extraction. The map is also missing the direct impacts these locations have on the people living nearby, especially those suffering from the environmental impacts of constant mining or water and energy consumption by data centers. This connects to Data Feminism because the ones who hold the power-tech companies in the global north-are the ones who get to decide what information about AI is shown, and what is buried.
