What It Means to Be Human in the Age of AI

Final Reflection

Prompt

What does it mean to be human in the age of AI?

Reflection

In the age of AI, being human requires an awareness of ethical decisions made when generating and using AI material. Right now, AI is easy to access, has few limitations, and is mostly free to the general public. People can create photorealistic images of others or spend a few hours programming something that may have previously taken days. But blindly using AI to create and provide solutions removes the human side from making. Instead, it begins to introduce questions of ownership, ethics, and originality.

When you send a prompt, countless invisible factors instantly come into play. Servers burn energy and create heat processing a response, tech companies collect data, and your conversation is moderated when necessary. On top of that, there's the work that went into building the infrastructure for you to prompt within. As Crawford argues and as is reflected in my footprint visualization make, AI is not developed in a vacuum. Instead, it has a profound global environmental impact that comes from data mining, transportation, and the space demands of data centers. Its design is a result of years of extraction and exploitation of our planet, our data, and our people.

Much of this impact is out of our control. If you decide to stop using AI, companies will continue building and exploiting. Your prompt, or lack thereof, will not save the world. But the humanity in this ethical decision comes into play when we, as a collective, become more aware and conscious of these choices. If everyone takes the time to consider whether they can ask AI for something or if they could pay an artist or Google it instead, and everyone works to spread awareness of the impacts of AI, we can begin to more critically engage with the mindful and human side of AI generation.

On top of that, AI is trained off of existing human creativity. There is a line between using AI in a remixing content-creation process, like the GIF in make 4, and solely taking the outputs of AI without refinement. AI is an algorithm, and generates responses from a collection of unattributed data. I believe that only using this data and the raw responses it outputs causes our creations to lose their humanity. When humans create, they never do so without inspiration or outside impact, much like how AI uses its training data. But the difference lies in how humans manipulate their inspirations, incorporating unique ideas and experiences of their own. As Bender writes, AI is a "stochastic parrot." It only takes in linguistic patterns and repeats them, without true thought behind them. Its patterns can be biased and harmful, often utilizing the easily accessible data from white and Western cultures. This lacks the humanity and originality of creation.

Therefore, when humans use AI to create, they must be critical and mindful of its outputs. The only way to hold ethical humanity in creation is through careful remixing and evaluation of the support AI offers them. The idea should be their own, and the execution should be carefully guided. In that sense, I believe that AI is a tool more than a co-creator. Since AI cannot think or be original, humans have to be willing to accept the full responsibility of their creation, acknowledge the biases it may contain, and take steps to input their own creativity. Without doing so, they themselves become stochastic parrots.

This ties closely with both my creation process throughout this semester, and the themes of critical making argued by Ratto. In the age of AI, creation becomes centered on the process rather than the output. Anyone could ask AI to generate an image of a puppy. That artwork becomes human and meaningful when the process is thought of critically, with an awareness of its impacts and a mindfulness of the data used. It becomes creative when a person implements their own ideas and vision, maybe in designing a background, creating a narrative, or sharing their own photos to be edited. When I have generated my makes utilizing AI, I have tried to keep my process as human as possible. I transitioned from believing that AI is a harmful tool that steals ideas, to seeing it as a support system that brings new possibilities into creation. It allows me to experiment and commentate on technological boundaries or speed up relatively arbitrary parts of a creative process. Focusing AI use on critical, aware, and ethical making is the only way to be human in the age of AI.
